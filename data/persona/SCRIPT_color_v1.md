# Video #1 - DRAFT script: "The Referee Sees Red"

Scope: wide (sports -> uniforms -> court -> dating -> the twist), skepticism woven in as voice.
~1,350 words. Dry-witty, mechanism-first, honest. Section breaks map to the cutaway engine.
STATUS: draft for owner review. Nothing rendered yet.

## 1. Cold open
Two athletes step onto the mat. Same height. Same weight. Same training. They fight, and it's close -
the kind of close where it could go either way. And then one of them wins. Now here's the strange part.
If we could rewind, swap the color of their gear, and run it again... the winner might change. Not the
fighting. Just the color. Same two people, same exact moves, and a different hand gets raised. That
sounds like superstition. It isn't. It's one of the more uncomfortable findings in sports science, and
once you see how it works, you start noticing it everywhere.

## 2. Red in nature
Start with a basic question: why would a color do anything at all? Because in nature, red is rarely a
decoration. It's a status report. A male mandrill's face goes a brighter red as he climbs the ranks, and
fades when he falls - the color is wired directly to his testosterone. He can't fake it. Zebra finches
size each other up by the red of their beaks, and the redder bird usually wins. Across primates, birds,
fish - red tends to mean the same thing. "I am dominant, and this is not the day to test me." So the
question isn't really why animals respond to red. It's whether we, in our gym shorts and our jerseys,
ever stopped.

## 3. The Olympic pattern
In 2004, two researchers went looking. They pulled the results from four combat sports at the Athens
Olympics - boxing, taekwondo, and two kinds of wrestling. The crucial detail: in these sports, athletes
are handed red or blue gear at random. Nobody chooses. It's a coin flip. If color did nothing, red
should win half the time. It didn't. Red won fifty-five percent of bouts. And in the closest, most
evenly matched fights - the ones that could tip either way - red won sixty-two percent. A coin that lands
heads sixty-two percent of the time is not a fair coin. So something was loading it. The only question
was what.

## 4. Two suspects
There were two suspects. Suspect one: the color changes the fighters. Maybe wearing red makes you feel a
little more dangerous, and facing red makes you flinch a little more - the mandrill effect, running quietly
in a human nervous system. Suspect two: the color changes the person keeping score. The fighters are
identical; the referee is the one who's biased. Here's the problem with telling these apart. In a normal
match, both could be true at once, and you'd never know which one tipped it. You need a way to freeze the
fighters and test only the judge.

## 5. The color-swap
Which is exactly what someone did. Take real taekwondo footage. Show it to forty-two experienced referees,
and have them score it. Then take the same clips - the same kicks, the same fighters, the same instant in
time - and digitally swap the colors, so the athlete who was in red is now in blue. Show those to referees
too. The performance is bit-for-bit identical. The only thing that changed is a color filter. The red
athletes scored about thirteen percent higher. Same kick. Same human throwing it. Thirteen percent more
points, for being wrapped in red. That settles the argument. You can't claim the fighters fought
differently, because it was the same footage. The bias wasn't on the mat. It was behind the scoring table,
in the eyes of people who'd have sworn they were being fair.

## 6. Black uniforms
And once you know to look for it, the pattern has a longer history. Years earlier, researchers went through
penalty records in American football and hockey, and found that teams who wore black were penalized more.
Not occasionally - consistently. Two teams switched to black uniforms mid-history, and their penalty
minutes climbed right after. Maybe black-clad players really do play dirtier. But then the researchers ran
the same trick: they filmed identical plays, once in white, once in black, and asked referees to judge.
The black version was rated more aggressive. Same play. Darker jersey. More flags. Red flatters you to the
judge. Black incriminates you. Either way, the person scoring isn't watching what you did - they're
watching what you wore.

## 7. The courtroom
Now move it somewhere the score actually matters. A courtroom is just another room where humans watch a
person and decide a number, except the number is years. And juries, it turns out, are not immune to the
packaging. Defendants who look the part - the wrong tattoos, the wrong clothes - are reliably judged more
dangerous and sentenced harder, for the same alleged crime. Lawyers have known this forever, which is why
defendants tend to show up in soft grays and quiet collars, never red. Now - be careful here. This is
softer science than the sports studies. It's mostly mock juries, not real verdicts, and the cleanest
findings are about tattoos and dress, not color specifically. But the machine underneath is the same one
we just watched mis-score a kick. It does not switch off because the stakes went up. If anything, it gets
better at hiding.

## 8. Romantic red
And then there's the place the science got a little drunk on itself. Around 2008, a famous set of studies
claimed that simply putting a woman in red made men rate her as more attractive - and that the men had no
idea it was happening. It's a great story. Red, the ancient signal, hijacking attraction in a coffee shop.
The trouble is, when other labs tried to repeat it, the effect got shy. Some found it; many didn't; the
big pooled analysis came back lukewarm. So I'm not going to tell you that wearing red will fix your dating
life - the honest answer is that this one is wobbling, and you should be suspicious of anyone who sells it
to you with confidence. Which is, itself, the lesson. The pull toward a tidy color story is strong enough
to bend the people studying it.

## 9. The twist
So where does that leave the red advantage - the original, solid one, the Olympic coin that landed red
sixty-two percent of the time? Here's the part I love. Somebody checked again, recently, with twenty more
years of data. And the advantage is gone. Today, red wins about fifty point five percent - a coin flip
again. What changed? The sports did. Taekwondo brought in electronic scoring and instant replay. The rules
got tighter, the human referee got quietly demoted, and a machine started counting the hits. And the moment
the biased judge stepped back... the magic evaporated. Think about what that means. If red had been making
the fighters genuinely better, the effect should have survived - the fighters are still human. It died the
instant we stopped trusting a person to score. Which tells you the advantage was never really in the red.
It was in us.

## 10. Closer
We'd like to believe we judge what's actually in front of us - the kick, the play, the person. Mostly, we
judge the wrapping, and then we invent a reason that sounds like merit. The good news, if you want one, is
that the cure is boring and it works: take the human out of the scoring, and the bias goes with them. The
bad news is that most of life doesn't have instant replay. Most of the time, it's just you, a color you
half-chose, and someone across the table who's certain they're being fair.
