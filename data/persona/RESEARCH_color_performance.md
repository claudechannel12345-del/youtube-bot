## 1. The study from the SAT (CONFIRMED - it's two studies)

The SAT passage was almost certainly adapted from the two canonical "red" studies. The owner's memory is
remarkably accurate (it was taekwondo, not wrestling, and "identical performances" rather than literal
draws - otherwise spot on).

- **Hill & Barton (2005), _Nature_ - "Red enhances human performance in contests."** 2004 Olympics, four
  combat sports (boxing, taekwondo, Greco-Roman + freestyle wrestling). Officials randomly assigned each
  competitor red or blue. **Red won 55% of bouts overall, 62% in close/evenly-matched bouts.** This is the
  owner's "red person has the advantage."
- **Hagemann, Strauss & Leissing (2008), _Psychological Science_ - "When the Referee Sees Red."** 42
  experienced taekwondo referees scored 11 video clips, then the SAME clips digitally color-swapped (red
  gear <-> blue gear). Same fighters, same moves. **Refs gave the "red" competitor ~13% more points.** This
  is EXACTLY the owner's "color-swap study where judges favored red."

The owner's "two reasons" = the two competing mechanisms the literature actually debates:
1. **Red changes the competitors** - the wearer feels more dominant / the opponent is intimidated (red =
   testosterone/dominance signal in primates; Hill & Barton's evolutionary framing).
2. **Red changes the judges** - referees perceive identical actions as stronger/more successful when in
   red (Hagemann). Bias in the eye of the scorer, not the body of the fighter.

## 2. THE TWIST (this is what makes it a video, and it's honest)

A **2025 meta-analysis by the same researchers** (Durham/Northumbria; _Scientific Reports_ 2024/25) looked
at 6,589 bouts across Olympics 1996-2020 + World Boxing 2005-2021. **Red now wins 50.5% overall, 51.5% in
close bouts - statistically indistinguishable from a coin flip.** The red advantage FADED.

Why it faded is the payoff: **electronic scoring + instant replay arrived** (taekwondo went electronic in
2012/2016), and rules were clarified - **less room for human referee interpretation.** When you take the
biased human judge out of the loop, the red advantage largely evaporates. That's strong evidence the
original effect lived mostly in **perception, not performance.** "It was never really about the fighters.
It was about the people watching them." Honest, surprising, and it resolves the two-suspects mystery.

## 3. Where else color/appearance hijacks judgment (the owner's "what else" expansion)

- **Black uniforms - Frank & Gilovich (1988), "The dark side of self- and social perception."** NFL + NHL
  teams in black uniforms drew significantly more penalties; refs rated IDENTICAL staged plays as more
  aggressive when the player wore black. Vancouver + Pittsburgh's penalty minutes JUMPED after switching to
  black in the late '70s. Same mechanism (biased scoring), different color. **Strongest parallel - use it.**
- **The courtroom - defendant clothing.** Mock-juror studies: a suspect in a black shirt is rated more
  aggressive and more likely guilty than the same person in light clothing; real courts deliberately dress
  defendants in neutral greys/browns and avoid red. (Softer/relatable; ties to owner's "charged for a
  crime.") Evidence is real but less airtight than the sports studies - present as suggestive, not proven.
- **Romantic red - Elliot & Niesta (2008), "Romantic Red."** Men rate the same woman as more attractive in
  red; men are unaware of the effect; red also reads as higher status. CAVEAT: a 2018 meta-analysis and
  registered replications are MIXED/weak - this one is shaky. If used, hedge it explicitly (good honesty
  beat, fits owner's "meeting new people").
- **Job-interview color advice** is mostly pop-psych / survey-based, not rigorous. Mention only as the
  "and then there's the stuff people *claim*" soft tier, or skip.

## 4. Competing-video check

No single dominant/viral YouTube video owns this exact framing. The "wear red to win" angle shows up in
blogs/listicles (Inc., Samford, Scientific American) and is mildly known. What is NOT saturated: the
**reframe** the owner instinctively reached for - "color doesn't improve you, it corrupts the judgment of
whoever's scoring you" - carried across sports -> court -> dating, **plus the 2025 fade as the proof.** No
Veritasium/Vox lock found. Differentiation is good if we lead with the judging-bias thesis + the twist,
NOT with the tired "lucky red" take.

## 5. Proposed framework (CGP-Grey-style, dry-witty, ~6-9 min)

Working titles: "Red Doesn't Make You Better. It Makes Them Worse." / "The Referee Sees Red" / "The Color
That Wins Your Arguments For You."

1. **Cold open** - two identical taekwondo fighters, same move, same instant. One scores. Only difference:
   gear color. (Hagemann color-swap as the hook.)
2. **The pattern** - Hill & Barton: across the Olympics, red just... wins more. 55% / 62% close. Set the
   mystery: why would a *color* win fights?
3. **Two suspects** - (a) red changes the fighter, (b) red changes the judge. Frame it as a whodunit (plays
   to the owner's "two reasons" instinct).
4. **The experiment that cracks it** - the color-swap referee study. Identical footage, +13% to red. The
   variable isn't the fighter. It's the eye scoring them.
5. **Zoom out** - this isn't a sports quirk. Black uniforms -> more penalties. Courts dress defendants to
   beat your instincts. Red reads as status/attraction. We're all quietly dressing for the judge.
6. **The twist** - then the machines took over scoring. And the red advantage died (50.5%, 2025). Remove the
   human, remove the magic. The effect was never in the fighter - it was in us.
7. **Closer** - dry line: we'd like to believe we judge what's actually in front of us. Mostly, we judge the
   wrapping. Fade.

Engine fit: heavy on comparison_stage (red vs blue panels), stat_stage (55% / 13% / 50.5%), quote_stage
(study lines), caption_punch (the two suspects, the twist), diagram (mechanism). Maps cleanly onto the
cutaway families we just finished.
